# C2 — Command and Control

## What it is

In **Apex Legends**, when Bloodhound drops their ult and the world goes red, every enemy footprint pulses on the map for the next 30 seconds. The squad rotates because someone, somewhere off-screen, is feeding them position data on a steady tick. That's the same shape as C2 — the **compromised host inside your network is the enemy squad, and somewhere off-map an operator is calling rotations over comms**. The host beacons out, the operator answers, the squad moves.

**Plain English:** C2 is the persistent channel a piece of malware uses to phone home, fetch instructions, and hand back stolen data. The implant on the box is the foot soldier. The C2 server is the IGL.

**Technical:** Command and Control is the post-exploitation communication channel between a compromised endpoint and an attacker-controlled infrastructure. It is the sixth phase of the [[Cyber Kill Chain]] and the **TA0011** tactic in [[MITRE ATT&CK]]. Without C2, an implant is deaf — no tasking, no exfil, no [[lateral movement]] coordination. Kill the channel, and the intrusion freezes.

## Why it matters

C2 detection is the single highest-value catch in a SOC. By the time you see C2 traffic, the attacker is already past initial access, past privilege escalation, and into the actions-on-objective window. But it's also the longest-lived signal — implants beacon for days, weeks, months. Every beacon is a chance to catch them. Miss it and you read about the breach in the news.

**Exam relevance:** Objective CS0-003 1.3 lists "Command and control" explicitly under common techniques for determining malicious activity. CompTIA expects you to recognize C2 traffic in packet captures, DNS logs, proxy logs, and EDR telemetry — and to know the toolset that exposes it.

## Key facts

### How C2 actually communicates

| Channel | What it looks like | Why attackers love it |
|---|---|---|
| **HTTPS** | Outbound 443 to a CDN or freshly-registered domain | Encrypted, blends with every browser tab |
| **DNS tunneling** | High volume of TXT/NULL queries to one domain, long labels | DNS rarely blocked, rarely inspected |
| **HTTP** | GET/POST to weird URI patterns, hardcoded User-Agent | Cheap, blends with web traffic |
| **ICMP tunneling** | Oversized ping payloads, regular cadence | Most orgs don't inspect ICMP payload |
| **Domain fronting** | TLS SNI says `cdn.com`, Host header says `evil.com` | Hides true destination behind trusted CDN |
| **Cloud abuse** | Beacons to Slack, Dropbox, GitHub, Pastebin APIs | C2 over allowlisted SaaS — the nasty one |
| **Custom TCP/UDP** | Non-standard ports, raw socket protocols | Easy to spot if you're watching; invisible if you aren't |

### Beaconing — the heartbeat

The defining trait of C2 is **periodicity**. The implant sleeps, wakes, calls home, sleeps again. Default Cobalt Strike was 60 seconds with 0% jitter — a metronome any analyst could spot. Modern operators set 8-hour sleep with 40% jitter to dodge frequency analysis.

What you hunt for in [[NetFlow]] or proxy logs:
- **Regular intervals** — connections every N seconds ±jitter to the same destination
- **Low data volume per beacon** — implant says "anything for me?" in a few hundred bytes
- **Long session counts to rare destinations** — the host has talked to `weatherapi-prod-7.xyz` 4,000 times in 48 hours and nothing else has
- **Beacon-then-burst** — small heartbeats followed by one large outbound transfer (the exfil)

*The boring repetition is the tell. Humans don't generate traffic that regular. Computers do.*

### Tools that expose C2

**[[Wireshark]]** — packet-level truth. Filter `tcp.stream eq X`, follow the stream, watch the conversation. Useful when you have full PCAP. Painful at scale.

**[[Packet capture]]** appliances (Zeek, Suricata, Arkime) — full-fidelity capture sitting on a SPAN port. Zeek's `conn.log` will surface long-connection beacons; `ssl.log` exposes JA3 hashes that fingerprint TLS clients. Cobalt Strike has known JA3s. So does Sliver. So does Metasploit's Meterpreter.

**[[SIEM]] log correlation** — proxy logs + DNS logs + EDR process events stitched into one timeline. The C2 hunt query: "show me every destination talked to by exactly one internal host, more than 50 times, with average payload under 2KB."

**[[EDR]]** — sees the process making the outbound call. `rundll32.exe` calling `connect()` to a random IP is a different conversation than Chrome doing the same.

**Strings** on the binary — `strings malware.exe | grep -iE 'http|\.com|\.xyz'` will often dump hardcoded C2 domains right out of the implant. Always run this before submitting to a sandbox.

**[[VirusTotal]]** — pivot on the file hash, the C2 domain, the IP. The Relations tab is the gold. One known-bad domain often pivots to twenty other samples and ten other domains.

**[[Joe Sandbox]] / [[Cuckoo Sandbox]]** — detonate the sample, watch what it talks to. Sandboxes produce a Network section that lists every DNS query, every HTTP request, every TLS handshake the malware attempted. That's your C2 indicator list, served on a plate. Modern malware checks for sandbox artifacts and refuses to detonate — Joe's anti-evasion is better than Cuckoo's, which is why teams pay for it.

**[[WHOIS]]** — when did the domain register? A domain registered 4 days ago that your accounting server is beaconing to is not a customer.

**[[AbuseIPDB]]** — community-reported abuse score on the destination IP. Fast triage signal.

### DNS as a C2 channel

DNS deserves its own section because nobody blocks it and almost nobody inspects it.

- **DNS tunneling** — encode payload data in subdomain labels: `aGVsbG8gd29ybGQ.evil.com`. Each query smuggles a few bytes. Slow but invisible.
- **DGA (Domain Generation Algorithm)** — implant generates 10,000 random-looking domains per day (`xkqplmnv.com`, `zvbnmkqp.com`); operator registers only the one for today. Defenders can't block what doesn't exist yet.
- **Fast flux** — single domain rotates through hundreds of IPs in hours.

Detection: high volume of NXDOMAIN responses (DGA misses), unusually long query labels, high entropy in subdomain strings (use [[regular expressions]] or a Shannon-entropy script in [[Python]]).

### Common indicators of compromise

| Layer | What you see |
|---|---|
| **Network** | Beaconing cadence, rare-destination domains, JA3 hash match, [[DNS]] anomalies |
| **Endpoint** | Unusual parent-child process trees (Word spawning PowerShell spawning network call), scheduled tasks calling unknown URLs, registry Run keys |
| **Identity** | [[Abnormal account activity]], [[impossible travel]], service accounts logging in from workstations |
| **Logs** | Outbound proxy denials clustering on one host, repeated User-Agent strings not matching browser fleet |

### CompTIA exam traps

> **CompTIA exam trap:** C2 is **not** the initial infection. The Kill Chain order is Recon → Weaponization → Delivery → Exploitation → Installation → **Command and Control** → Actions on Objectives. If a question describes a phishing email landing in a mailbox, that's Delivery. C2 starts when the installed implant first reaches back out.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** Encrypted traffic to a known-good destination is not automatically safe. **Domain fronting** and cloud-SaaS C2 (Slack, Discord, Dropbox APIs) blend into allowlisted destinations. The exam answer is to inspect the **process originating the connection** and the **cadence**, not the destination reputation alone.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** A beacon at 8-hour intervals with jitter looks like nothing in a one-hour log window. Long-tail analysis — 7 to 30 days of proxy logs — is the only way to spot slow beacons. CompTIA will offer "check the last hour of logs" as a wrong answer.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** [[Sandboxing]] is **not** authoritative for C2 detection if the malware is sandbox-aware. The right answer when a sample "did nothing" in the sandbox is to re-detonate with anti-evasion features enabled, on a host with realistic uptime and user artifacts — not to conclude the file is benign.

### Interpreting suspicious traffic — the analyst workflow

1. **Source the alert** — which sensor fired? EDR? IDS? SIEM correlation rule?
2. **Identify the host and the process** — `lsass.exe` and `chrome.exe` make outbound calls for legitimate reasons. `notepad.exe` does not.
3. **Pivot on the destination** — WHOIS age, VirusTotal reputation, AbuseIPDB score, passive DNS history
4. **Pull the [[PCAP]]** if available — look at payload, TLS fingerprint, HTTP headers
5. **Hash the suspect binary** — submit hash (not file, hash first) to VirusTotal
6. **Detonate in sandbox** — if hash is unknown, controlled detonation gives you the C2 indicator list
7. **Hunt enterprise-wide** — same destination, same hash, same JA3 across other hosts? You now have scope.

## SOC reality

- The 3am alert reads `Beacon-Like Traffic Detected: 192.168.42.17 → 185.x.x.x, 847 connections, mean interval 60.2s, stddev 4.1s`. Your first move is to confirm the destination isn't a corporate poll service before you wake the IR lead. It usually isn't. Sometimes it is, and you've learned why we tune.
- L1 acknowledges, validates the host owner, checks the running process via EDR, and pulls 7 days of proxy history for that host. If the destination is unknown and the process is suspicious, escalate to L2 — do not block at the firewall yet. Blocking the C2 tells the attacker you're watching, and they pivot to a backup channel you haven't found.
- The IR lead asks: *"How many hosts are talking to that destination? When was first contact? What did they exfil? Is the implant persistent — scheduled task, service, Run key?"* If you can't answer scope and persistence, you don't yet have containment.
- Never tell leadership "we've blocked the C2 so we're safe." You've blocked **one** C2. Real adversaries deploy 2–5 channels per intrusion. The right phrasing is *"primary channel severed, hunting for secondaries, containment in progress."*
- Handoff: L1 triages and scopes → L2 confirms malicious and pulls forensic artifacts → IR team coordinates containment (network isolation, credential rotation, host re-image) → threat intel pivots IoCs across the fleet → legal/exec get briefed only when scope and impact are nailed down.
