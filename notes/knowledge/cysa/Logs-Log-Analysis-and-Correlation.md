# Logs, Log Analysis, and Correlation

## What it is

In **Tekken**, when you lose a round to a player who looks like they're reading your inputs, you go to **Replay & Tips** and scrub the match frame by frame. Every input you threw — every `df+2`, every sidestep, every blocked launcher — is recorded with frame data. You watch yourself whiff a punish at frame 14 when the opening was 12 frames. You watch your opponent step-cancel into Heat at the exact moment you committed to a string. The match is over, but the inputs are still there, timestamped, queryable, undeniable. *That's exactly what logs do — they're the input history of every system, written to disk, scrubable after the fight is over.*

A **log** is a timestamped record of an event generated by a system, application, service, or piece of infrastructure. **Log analysis** is the act of querying, parsing, and interpreting those records to determine what happened. **Correlation** is the act of stitching events from multiple sources into a single timeline — the network log shows the outbound connection, the endpoint log shows the process that opened it, the auth log shows the account that spawned the process. Three logs, one story.

The SOC lives or dies on this loop. No logs, no investigation. Bad logs, wrong investigation. Uncorrelated logs, you see three separate alerts instead of one intrusion.

## Why it matters

CySA+ Objective 1.2 is the longest single objective on the exam because every indicator of malicious activity ultimately shows up as a log entry somewhere. The exam will hand you a scenario — a Windows event, a firewall denial, a process tree — and ask what it indicates. If you can't read the log, you can't pass the question.

In the SOC, log analysis is the daily job. Tier 1 lives in the SIEM console. Tier 2 pivots between SIEM, EDR, and raw log stores. IR teams reconstruct attack timelines from log artifacts months after the fact. Without logs you have rumor; with logs you have evidence. Courts know the difference. So do auditors.

## Key facts

### Where logs come from

| Source | What it tells you | Common format |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating system** | Auth events, process creation, privilege use, service changes | Windows Event Log (.evtx), syslog, journald |
| **Application** | App-specific events — web requests, DB queries, errors, transactions | App-defined; often JSON or CLF |
| **Network device** | Allow/deny decisions, flow records, routing changes | syslog, NetFlow/IPFIX, sFlow |
| **Security tool** | Detections, blocks, quarantines, scan results | Vendor-specific; often CEF or LEEF |
| **Cloud platform** | API calls, IAM changes, resource creation | CloudTrail, Azure Activity, GCP Audit |
| **Endpoint (EDR)** | Process trees, file writes, registry changes, network connections | Vendor telemetry stream |

The **scope** question — localized or organizational — is answered by which logs lit up. One endpoint's Sysmon firing on `powershell.exe -enc` is a host-level incident. A hundred endpoints' Sysmon firing on the same encoded blob inside the same hour is a campaign.

### What you're actually looking for (CompTIA 1.2 by category)

**Network-related indicators:**
- **Bandwidth consumption** — sustained outbound throughput from a host that normally pushes kilobytes. Classic exfiltration signal.
- **Beaconing** — small, regular outbound connections at fixed intervals (e.g., every 60s ± jitter) to the same destination. C2 heartbeat.
- **Unexpected outbound** — host talking to a country, ASN, or domain category it has never touched before.
- **Irregular peer-to-peer** — workstations talking to each other on SMB, RDP, or WinRM when they shouldn't. Lateral movement.
- **Scans and sweeps** — one source, many destinations, sequential ports or sequential IPs. Recon.
- **Activity on unexpected ports** — DNS on TCP/53 to a non-resolver host, HTTPS on port 8443 to a fresh domain, SSH outbound from a workstation.
- **Rogue devices on the network** — DHCP leases or ARP entries for MACs that aren't in your asset inventory.
- **Unusual traffic spikes** — deviation from the host's or segment's baseline.

**Host-related indicators:**
- **Processor consumption** — sustained high CPU on a process that shouldn't be working hard. Cryptominer signature.
- **Memory consumption** — unexplained RAM pressure, especially from a service account.
- **Drive capacity consumption** — disk filling up without a known cause. Staging directory for exfil, or ransomware encrypting before exfil.
- **Unauthorized software** — new binaries on disk that didn't come through the deployment pipeline.
- **Malicious processes** — `powershell.exe` spawned by `winword.exe`, `cmd.exe` spawned by `w3wp.exe`, `rundll32.exe` with a non-system DLL path.
- **Abnormal OS process behavior** — `lsass.exe` being read by a non-system process (credential dump), `svchost.exe` running from a user profile path.
- **Unauthorized scheduled tasks** — new tasks in `\Microsoft\Windows\` namespaces that aren't Microsoft's.
- **Registry changes or anomalies** — new `Run` keys, new services, new image file execution options.
- **File system changes** — writes to `%TEMP%`, `%APPDATA%`, `\ProgramData\`, or alternate data streams.
- **Introduction of new accounts** — local administrators created on workstations, service accounts spawned outside IAM workflow.
- **Unauthorized privileges** — token elevation events, group membership changes, especially `Domain Admins` or `Enterprise Admins`.

**Application-related indicators:**
- **Anomalous activity** — login from impossible geography, transaction volume spike, API call patterns outside the app's normal envelope.
- **Unexpected output** — error rates spiking, responses leaking stack traces, query results returning columns that shouldn't be queryable.
- **Service interruption** — crashes, restarts, OOMs that map to attempted exploitation.
- **Unauthorized changes** — config files modified outside change windows, new admin users in the app's own RBAC.
- **Data exfiltration** — large result sets, unusual export endpoints, off-hours SELECT * from sensitive tables.
- **Social engineering signals in app logs** — password reset floods, helpdesk ticket spikes, MFA fatigue (push approval after dozens of denies).
- **Obfuscated links** — URL shorteners, IDN homographs, base64 in query strings, hex-encoded redirects.

### Correlation — turning events into incidents

A single log entry is a data point. Correlation makes it a story.

**Vertical correlation** stitches the same host's logs across layers: firewall log (outbound connection) ↔ EDR log (which process opened the socket) ↔ auth log (which user ran the process) ↔ email gateway log (which attachment dropped the binary). One host, four data sources, one timeline.

**Horizontal correlation** stitches across hosts: the same IoC (hash, domain, IP, mutex) appearing on multiple endpoints within a window. This is how you scope a campaign from one alert.

**Temporal correlation** is the easiest to miss. Events that look unrelated by source but cluster in time — failed logins on Host A at 02:14, successful login on Host B at 02:15, scheduled task created on Host B at 02:16 — that's an attack chain, not three independent oddities.

### Analysis methods

- **SIEM** — [[SIEM]] platforms (Splunk, Sentinel, QRadar, Elastic Security) ingest, normalize, and correlate at scale. Detection logic lives in correlation rules and analytics.
- **Log aggregation stacks** — ELK / OpenSearch for the homelab and budget-conscious SOC. Less detection content out of the box; more flexibility.
- **Manual review** — raw log digging when the SIEM doesn't have the source onboarded, or when you don't trust the parser. Required skill, not optional.
- **Trend analysis** — comparing current activity to historical baselines. The host that suddenly pushes 4GB outbound at 3am didn't do that last Tuesday.

### CompTIA exam traps

> **CompTIA exam trap:** Beaconing vs scanning vs exfiltration on the same wire. Beaconing is *small, regular, persistent* outbound. Scanning is *one-to-many, sequential, short-lived*. Exfiltration is *sustained, high-volume, often to cloud storage or an unfamiliar destination*. CompTIA will describe traffic and ask which it is — pay attention to **packet size**, **regularity**, and **destination count**.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** "Unexpected output" is not the same as "service interruption." Unexpected output = the app returns something wrong (stack trace, wrong dataset, leaked field). Service interruption = the app stops responding or crashes. CompTIA tests the distinction.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** Scope vs impact. **Scope** = how many systems / users / segments are involved. **Impact** = what business function is affected and how bad. A ransomware hit on one isolated test VM has small scope and small impact. A credential compromise on one HR admin has small scope and large impact. Don't conflate them.

> **CompTIA exam trap:** Correlation is not the same as aggregation. **Aggregation** = collecting logs in one place. **Correlation** = identifying relationships between events across sources. A pile of logs in ELK is aggregation; a rule that fires when "failed login + new account creation + privilege change" happens to the same user inside 10 minutes is correlation.

## SOC reality

- The 3am alert reads: *"Beaconing detected — host WKS-4471 → 185.x.x.x every 63s ± 4s for 47 minutes — confidence: high."* You don't trust "confidence: high." You pull the EDR process tree, the firewall log, and the DNS log before you touch the keyboard for containment.
- L1's first action is **never** isolate. It's **acknowledge, validate, pivot**. Pull the host's last 24 hours of process telemetry. Check if the destination is in a known threat-intel feed. Look for the same destination on other hosts. *Premature isolation tips off the adversary and burns your visibility into their next move.*
- The IR lead asks three questions, in order: "**What's the scope, what's the impact, and what evidence have we preserved?**" If you can't answer all three, you're not ready to brief.
- Never tell leadership "it's just one host" until horizontal correlation across the fleet has run and come back clean. The exfil tool you found on Host A was probably dropped by the same loader on Hosts B and C — you just haven't queried for it yet.
- Handoff: L1 validates and enriches → L2 scopes via correlation → IR team scopes via threat hunting → legal/exec brief once scope and impact are bounded. Skip a layer and you create gaps in the chain of custody.
