# Interfaces and Cables

## What it is

In Stardew Valley, you don't water your crops with your fists — you pick the right tool for the job. A basic Watering Can soaks one tile at a time and works fine for a starter farm, but once your operation sprawls across the Forest map you upgrade to Iridium and start hitting 18 tiles per swing. That's exactly what Ethernet cabling does — different physical media for different farm sizes, where "farm size" means speed, distance, and how much gold you're willing to spend at Clint's.

Ethernet itself is the ruleset — **IEEE 802.3**, ratified in **1983** — that defines how bits get shoved across a wire. Everything below is just different physical implementations of that same ruleset, the way every season in Stardew still runs on the same 28-day calendar even though spring crops and winter crops behave nothing alike.

The two big families:
- **Copper (UTP)** — unshielded twisted pair, the standard RJ45 cable plugged into your PC right now.
- **Fiber** — glass strands carrying pulses of light, used when copper's 100-meter leash isn't enough.

## Why it matters

Pick the wrong cable and your "10 Gbps" link silently negotiates down to a gigabit, or worse, the link won't come up at all. It's the same frustration as buying a 240Hz monitor and forgetting you're still on an HDMI 1.4 cable — the spec on the box means nothing if the physical layer can't carry it.

Cable choice also dictates your building's layout. Copper caps at 100 m, so anything beyond that — between buildings on a campus, across a city, between data centers — needs fiber. This is why every datacenter looks like a bowl of yellow and aqua spaghetti.

## Key facts

### UTP (Copper)

UTP is the bread-and-butter cable. The "twisted" part isn't aesthetic — pairs are twisted together so that electromagnetic interference (EMI) hitting both wires gets cancelled out at the receiver. It's noise-cancelling headphones, but for electrons.

- **100 m max length** on every copper Ethernet standard. Past that, signal degrades. No exceptions, no "but my cable is really nice."
- **Cat5** — up to 100 Mbps. Legacy.
- **Cat5e** — 1 Gbps. The minimum you should ever pull through a wall today.
- **Cat6** — 10 Gbps. What you want for any new run.

### Speed standards (the BASE-T family)

Each speed has its own IEEE designation. "BASE-T" = baseband signaling over twisted pair.

| Standard | Speed | IEEE |
|---|---|---|
| 10BASE-T | 10 Mbps | 802.3i |
| 100BASE-T | 100 Mbps | 802.3u |
| 1000BASE-T | 1 Gbps | 802.3ab |
| 10GBASE-T | 10 Gbps | 802.3an |

10/100 only use 2 of the 4 pairs. **1000BASE-T and 10GBASE-T light up all four pairs simultaneously** — like switching from a single-stick controller scheme to using all four bumpers and triggers at once to push more data per second.

### Straight-through vs. Crossover

Pin assignments are like matchmaking roles in Marvel Rivals — devices are either Vanguards (transmit on 1,2) or Strategists (transmit on 3,6), and you can't have two of the same role talking on the same lane.

- **PC and Router** transmit on pins **1, 2** and receive on **3, 6**.
- **Switch** transmits on **3, 6** and receives on **1, 2**.
- **Straight-through cable** — pin 1 to pin 1, pin 2 to pin 2, etc. Works when the two devices are *different roles* (PC ↔ Switch, Router ↔ Switch). Supports full-duplex.
- **Crossover cable** — transmit pins are swapped to receive pins on the other end. Needed when both devices are the *same role* (PC ↔ PC, Switch ↔ Switch, Router ↔ Router).
- **Auto MDI-X** — modern devices auto-detect and flip pins internally. The cable type stops mattering, the way modern controllers auto-pair instead of making you fiddle with a sync button.

### SFP Modules

SFP (Small Form-factor Pluggable) is the modular weapon attachment system of networking. Instead of a switch having permanently soldered ports, it has empty slots — slap in a copper SFP for a short run, swap in a fiber SFP for a long run. Same gun, different barrel.

- **Hot-swappable** — pull and replace while the device is powered on.
- Supports **both fiber and copper** media.
- Lets one switch model serve a dozen different deployment scenarios.

### Fiber Optic Standards

Fiber comes in two flavors based on core diameter:

**Multimode Fiber (MMF)**
- Core ~**50 µm** — wide enough that light bounces around in multiple "modes."
- Driven by cheap **LEDs**.
- **OM1/OM2** — up to ~2 km at lower speeds.
- **OM3/OM4** — up to ~550 m at 10 Gbps.
- Aqua-colored jackets (OM3/OM4). The budget option for in-building runs.

**Single-mode Fiber (SMF)**
- Core **8–10 µm** — so narrow only one mode of light propagates straight down it.
- Driven by **lasers**, more expensive.
- Reaches **100 km or more** with high-power optics.
- Yellow jacket. The sniper rifle of cabling.

**Fiber Ethernet standards:**

| Standard | Speed | Media | Distance | IEEE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1000BASE-LX | 1 Gbps | MMF / SMF | 550 m / 5 km | 802.3z |
| 10GBASE-SR | 10 Gbps | MMF | 400 m | 802.3ae |
| 10GBASE-LR | 10 Gbps | SMF | 10 km | 802.3ae |
| 10GBASE-ER | 10 Gbps | SMF | 30 km | 802.3ae |

Mnemonic: **S**R = **S**hort, **L**R = **L**ong, **E**R = **E**xtended. Like Pokémon evolutions but for how far your photons can yeet themselves.
