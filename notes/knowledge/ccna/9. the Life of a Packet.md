# The Life of a Packet

## What it is

A package being shipped from Brooklyn to Los Angeles doesn't get a new shipping label at every warehouse — the "From" and "To" addresses stay the same the whole trip. But the truck carrying it changes constantly: a local van picks it up, hands it to a long-haul semi, which hands it to another local van for delivery. The package is the **packet** (Layer 3, with IP addresses that never change). The truck is the **frame** (Layer 2, with MAC addresses that get swapped at every single handoff).

That's the entire mental model. A packet is the durable thing. A frame is the disposable wrapper that only exists to get the packet across one network segment.

When your computer at 192.168.1.11/24 wants to talk to 192.168.3.11, it does the math: "Is 192.168.3.11 in my subnet 192.168.1.0/24? No." So it can't deliver directly — it hands the packet to its **default gateway** (the router) and says "you figure it out." Same energy as DoorDash: you don't drive to the restaurant yourself, you hand the job to someone whose entire role is moving things between places.

## Why it matters

This is the heartbeat of every network conversation you've ever had. Every Discord message, every Counter-Strike 2 match, every Netflix stream — they all live this packet life. Understanding it means you can debug almost anything: why your ping fails, why a misconfigured gateway breaks everything, why ARP poisoning is so devastating, why a switch with a poisoned MAC table behaves like a confused NPC.

It also explains a subtle truth that trips people up: **switches are blind to IP addresses, routers are blind to MAC addresses past the current hop**. They live in different layers and only one of them (the router) actually knows where the destination is in the grand scheme. Switches are just ushers in a hallway — they know which door (port) each MAC walked in through and that's it.

## Key facts

### The two address types and their scopes

- **IP address scope = the entire internet.** Like your gamertag in an MMO — globally unique, follows you everywhere, used for end-to-end identity.
- **MAC address scope = one network segment only.** Like the seat number you got assigned in a single Uber ride. Useless once you're out of that car.
- The **source and destination IP never change** from origin to final destination. The shipping label stays put.
- The **source and destination MAC change at every hop**. New truck, new driver, every leg.

### The forwarding process at each hop

Every router in the path runs the exact same loop, like an enemy AI routine in Helldivers 2:

1. **Strip the Layer 2 header** — rip off the old shipping truck.
2. **Look up the destination IP in the routing table** — "where does this package need to go next?"
3. **ARP for the next hop's MAC** (if not cached) — "what's the license plate of the next truck?"
4. **Build a new frame** with the router's own MAC as source and the next hop's MAC as destination — load it onto the new truck.
5. Forward.

### ARP — the IP-to-MAC translator

- ARP maps **IP addresses → MAC addresses**. It's how a device asks "hey, who owns 192.168.1.1? I need your MAC to actually send you a frame."
- Before sending, a host **checks its ARP table first**. Cached lookups are like remembering a friend's Discord handle — no need to ask again.
- If there's no entry, the host **broadcasts an ARP request**: "WHO HAS 192.168.1.1?" — yelled into the entire local segment, like an @everyone ping.
- The **switch floods that broadcast out every port except the one it came in on** (a switch never echoes back to the sender).
- The owner of that IP replies with a **unicast ARP reply** — a quiet DM, not another @everyone.
- **Both sides update their ARP tables** after the exchange. Mutual contact-add.
- ARP table entries time out around **300 seconds** (~5 minutes).
- ARP tables live on **end hosts and routers** — anything that originates or rebuilds frames.

### MAC address tables — the switch's cheat sheet

- Switches maintain a **MAC address table** mapping **MAC addresses → switch ports**. "Player Beatrice is in seat 3, player Dante is in seat 7."
- Switches **learn MAC addresses by reading the source MAC** of every frame that arrives. They never learn from the destination — only from who's actually talking.
- Switches **never read Layer 3** — IP addresses are invisible to them. They're a Pokémon that only knows one move.
- MAC table entries time out between **5 and 300 seconds** depending on the platform.

### A worked example: 192.168.1.11 → 192.168.3.11

- Your PC sees the destination is **not in 192.168.1.0/24**, so it sends to its default gateway.
- It ARPs for the gateway's MAC (or pulls it from cache).
- The frame: **src MAC = your PC, dst MAC = gateway**. The packet inside: **src IP = 192.168.1.11, dst IP = 192.168.3.11**.
- The gateway router strips the frame, looks up 192.168.3.11 in its routing table, finds the next hop, ARPs for that next hop's MAC, builds a brand new frame.
- Frame now: **src MAC = router's outbound interface, dst MAC = next hop**. Packet IPs are unchanged.
- Repeat at every router until one of them sees "192.168.3.0/24 is **directly connected**" — meaning the destination is sitting on its local segment, no more hops needed.
- That final router ARPs for 192.168.3.11 itself and delivers the frame straight to it.

### Routing table vs. directly connected

- A router forwards based on the **destination IP** matched against its **routing table**.
- The **next-hop IP** decides who gets the frame on this leg; the **destination IP** decides the ultimate target.
- When the routing table says "directly connected," the router knows the target lives on the segment attached to that interface — no more next hops, time to ARP for the actual host.

### The return trip

- Reply traffic reuses the **already-cached ARP entries** on every device along the path. No new broadcasts needed (until timers expire).
- This is why the second ping in a series is almost always faster than the first — the first one paid the ARP tax for everyone.
