# Ethernet LAN Switching

## What it is

A LAN switch is the bouncer at a Discord server's voice channels — it knows which user is in which channel, and when someone @mentions a specific person, it routes the ping straight to them instead of blasting everyone. A **Local Area Network (LAN)** is a group of devices wired or Wi-Fi'd together inside one building or campus, and a **switch** is the device that shuffles Ethernet frames between them by reading MAC addresses off a lookup table.

The switch operates inside a **Layer 2 domain** — the boundary where forwarding decisions are made purely on MAC addresses, no IP routing involved. Think of it like a Counter-Strike 2 lobby: inside the lobby, players talk to each other directly by player name. Crossing to another lobby requires a different mechanism (a router). Inside? Just the switch and its MAC table.

The currency the switch deals in is the **Ethernet frame** — a structured packet with a header (who's it from, who's it for), a payload (the actual data), and a trailer (an integrity check). Every frame on your LAN looks the same on the wire; only the contents differ.

## Why it matters

Without switches, every device on a LAN would hear every other device's traffic — like an Among Us lobby where every "vent" sound plays for everyone in the entire server, not just the room. Hubs used to work that way and it was a disaster: collisions, wasted bandwidth, zero privacy.

Switches kill that noise. They learn which MAC address lives behind which physical port and forward frames only where they need to go. That's why your gigabit home network actually delivers something close to gigabit between two devices — the switch isn't broadcasting your Steam download to your roommate's PS5.

Understanding frame structure, MAC learning, and ARP is the foundation for everything else: VLANs, spanning tree, security features like port security and DHCP snooping. Skip this and the rest of networking is just memorizing commands.

## Key facts

### The Ethernet Frame (the package)

A frame is like a shipped Amazon box: there's a label on the outside (header), the product inside (payload), and a tamper seal (trailer). Field by field:

- **Preamble** — 7 bytes of `10101010...` pattern. It's the "3, 2, 1, GO" countdown before a speedrun — lets the receiver's clock sync up with the sender's before the real data arrives.
- **Start Frame Delimiter (SFD)** — 1 byte, value `10101011`. The pattern flips on the last bit, signaling "frame starts NOW."
- **Destination MAC** — 6 bytes. Who the frame is for.
- **Source MAC** — 6 bytes. Who sent it.
- **Type/Length** — 2 bytes. If the value is **≤1500**, it's a length field (how many bytes of payload). If **≥1536**, it's an **EtherType** identifying what's inside. `0x0800` = IPv4, `0x86DD` = IPv6.
- **Payload** — 46 to 1500 bytes of encapsulated upper-layer data (usually an IP packet).
- **Padding** — 0 to 20 bytes added if the payload is shorter than 46 bytes. Like stuffing packing peanuts in a half-empty box so it meets the minimum shipping size.
- **Frame Check Sequence (FCS)** — 4 bytes. A CRC-32 checksum. The receiver recomputes it; if it doesn't match, the frame gets dropped. No retransmission at this layer — that's TCP's job upstairs.

**Frame size rules:**
- Minimum frame size: **64 bytes**
- Maximum standard frame size: **1518 bytes**
- **Jumbo frames** exceed 1518, usually around **9000 bytes**. Used in storage networks and data centers where you want fewer, bigger frames to cut overhead — like carrying one giant Costco haul instead of ten grocery bags.

### MAC Addresses (the player IDs)

A MAC address is the unique gamertag burned into your network card at the factory. 48 bits total, written as six hex pairs like `00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E`.

- **OUI (Organizationally Unique Identifier)** — first 24 bits. Assigned by IEEE to the manufacturer. Apple, Intel, Cisco each have their own OUI prefixes. You can literally Google a MAC prefix and find out who made the card.
- **NIC field** — last 24 bits. The manufacturer's unique serial for that specific card.

### The CAM Table (the switch's memory)

The **CAM table** (Content-Addressable Memory), also called the MAC address table, is the switch's cheat sheet: "MAC `AA:BB:CC:...` lives behind port Gi0/3." The switch builds this table by watching the **source MAC** of every incoming frame and noting which port it came in on.

- **Dynamic entries** age out after **5 minutes** of inactivity. Like a Tinder match unmatching you for ghosting.
- **Static entries** are manually configured and never expire.

**Forwarding behavior:**
- **Known unicast** — destination MAC is in the table → forward only to that one port. Surgical.
- **Unknown unicast** — destination MAC isn't in the table → flood out every port except the one it came in on. The switch is basically yelling "anyone seen this guy?" The real recipient replies, the switch learns, and future frames go direct.
- **Broadcast** (`FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF`) — flooded to every port except the source. This is the @everyone ping.

### ARP (matching IP to MAC)

Your computer knows the IP it wants to talk to but needs the MAC to actually build the frame. **ARP (Address Resolution Protocol)** is how it asks.

- **ARP Request** — a **broadcast** that says "Who has 10.0.0.5? Tell 10.0.0.10." Every device on the LAN hears it.
- **ARP Reply** — a **unicast** from the IP owner: "10.0.0.5 is at MAC `00:1A:...`." Only the asker gets it.

The result lives in the **ARP table**, with three columns: Internet Address (IP), Physical Address (MAC), and Type (static or dynamic).

### Ping (the connectivity test)

**Ping** sends **ICMP Echo Requests** and listens for **Echo Replies** — like pinging a teammate in Apex Legends to see if they're alive. It also reports **round-trip time (RTT)**, which is your latency. High ping in a game and high ping on a network mean the same thing: your packets are taking too long to get back.

### Useful commands

- `show mac address-table` — dump every learned MAC and its port. The switch's full memory.
- `clear mac address-table dynamic` — wipe all dynamically learned entries. Useful after moving cables around.
- `clear mac address-table dynamic address <mac>` — surgical removal of one entry.
- `clear mac address-table dynamic interface <int>` — clear everything learned on a specific port.
- `arp -a` — on a host, displays the local ARP cache.
