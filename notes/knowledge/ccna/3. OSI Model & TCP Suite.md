# OSI Model & TCP Suite

## What it is

The OSI model is the seven-floor apartment building your data lives in on its way across the internet. Each floor has one job, and your packet rides the elevator down through every floor on the way out, then back up through every floor on the way in. If a floor is broken, nothing above it works — kind of like how if your Wi-Fi card dies, it doesn't matter how fancy your browser is.

The seven floors, top to bottom:

- **Layer 7 — Application**: the actual app you touch (Chrome, Discord, Steam).
- **Layer 6 — Presentation**: the translator and the bouncer. Handles character encoding, compression, and encryption.
- **Layer 5 — Session**: the doorman who opens, maintains, and closes the conversation. Authentication and sync live here.
- **Layer 4 — Transport**: the delivery service. TCP (signature required) or UDP (chuck it on the porch).
- **Layer 3 — Network**: the GPS. IP addresses and routing between networks.
- **Layer 2 — Data Link**: the local street signs. MAC addresses, frames, switches.
- **Layer 1 — Physical**: the actual wire, fiber, or radio wave. Raw bits.

The TCP/IP Suite is the stripped-down, real-world version that the internet actually runs on. It collapses the OSI model into a smaller stack: **Application, Transport, Internet, and Link**. Same job, fewer floors. OSI is the textbook diagram; TCP/IP is the working build.

## Why it matters

Troubleshooting without the OSI model is like trying to debug a Cyberpunk 2077 quest without knowing whether the issue is your save file, your GPU driver, or your ISP. Layering lets you isolate. Cable unplugged? Layer 1. Wrong VLAN? Layer 2. Can't reach the gateway? Layer 3. Firewall blocking port 443? Layer 4. TLS handshake failing? Layer 6.

It also matters for security. Every layer is its own attack surface. ARP poisoning lives at Layer 2. IP spoofing at Layer 3. SYN floods at Layer 4. SQL injection at Layer 7. A defender who doesn't think in layers will miss half the attacks.

## Key facts

### Layer 7 — Application
Where the apps and their port numbers live. Ports are like apartment numbers on a building — the IP gets you to the building, the port gets you to the right door.

- **HTTP — port 80**: plain web traffic, no lock on the door.
- **HTTPS — port 443**: web traffic with the lock (TLS).
- **FTP — ports 20/21**: file transfer. Port 21 is the conversation, port 20 is the actual file moving.
- **SMTP — ports 25/587**: sending mail. 25 is the legacy port, 587 is the modern submission port.
- **POP3 — port 110**: pull mail down and (usually) delete it from the server. Like clearing your Steam download queue.
- **IMAP — port 143**: keep mail synced across devices. Like Discord — same messages on your phone and PC.
- **DNS — port 53 (TCP and UDP)**: translates `youtube.com` into an IP. UDP for quick lookups, TCP for big zone transfers.
- **SSH — port 22**: encrypted remote shell. Watch Dogs-style remote access, except legal.

### Layer 6 — Presentation
The format-fixer between raw bytes and what the app expects.

- Handles **character encoding** (ASCII, UTF-8) — making sure an emoji typed on iOS shows up the same on Android.
- Handles **compression** — shrinking a file like a ZIP before it travels.
- Handles **encryption** — TLS lives here in spirit.

### Layer 5 — Session
Opens the conversation, keeps it alive, closes it cleanly.

- Manages **authentication and synchronization** — the login screen and the "still there?" pings.
- **NetBIOS**: legacy Windows session establishment. How old Windows boxes find each other on a LAN.
- **RPC (Remote Procedure Call)**: lets a program on Host A call a function on Host B like it's local. The networking equivalent of summoning a co-op partner into your game session.

### Layer 4 — Transport
The delivery contract.

- **TCP — connection-oriented and reliable**. Three-way handshake, acknowledgements, retransmission. Sends **segments**. Reliability comes with overhead — like ranked matchmaking, slower but fair.
- **UDP — connectionless and unreliable**. No handshake, no retries. Sends **datagrams**. Faster than TCP. Used for voice, video, gaming — anything where a dropped packet matters less than a late one. CS2 isn't going to retransmit a shot from two seconds ago; it just wants the next one.

### Layer 3 — Network
Routing between networks. Data unit: **packet**.

- **IPv4**: four-byte addressing (e.g., `192.168.1.10`). About 4.3 billion addresses, long since exhausted.
- **IPv6**: sixteen-byte addressing. Effectively unlimited.
- **ICMP**: error reporting and diagnostics. The protocol behind `ping` and `traceroute`. When a router can't forward your packet, ICMP is the "destination unreachable" sticky note.
- **OSPF**: interior gateway protocol — routing inside one organization's network. Like minimap pathing inside your own base.
- **BGP**: exterior gateway protocol — routing between organizations across the internet. The protocol that decides how your traffic gets from a Limbo Capital office in one country to a Styx Networking server in another.

### Layer 2 — Data Link
Local delivery on the same network segment. Data unit: **frame**.

- Two sublayers:
  - **LLC (Logical Link Control)**: error handling and flow control.
  - **MAC (Media Access Control)**: physical addressing and deciding who gets to talk on the wire.
- **MAC addresses** are the hardware ID burned into your NIC — like a gamertag stamped on the controller itself.
- **Ethernet**: the dominant LAN media access protocol.
- **ARP**: resolves an IP to a MAC. "I know Beatrice's username, but what's her actual account ID?"
- **PPP**: point-to-point links. Two endpoints, one wire.
- **VLAN**: logical segmentation. One physical switch, multiple isolated networks — like splitting a Discord server into channels nobody else can see.

### Layer 1 — Physical
The actual medium. Data unit: **bits** — raw 1s and 0s.

- **Twisted pair (Cat5e/Cat6)**: standard Ethernet copper.
- **Fiber optic**: long-haul, high-speed, light through glass. The backbone of the internet.
- **Wireless**: Wi-Fi (802.11) and Bluetooth. No cable, just radio.

### TCP/IP Suite (the practical version)
- Four layers: **Application, Transport, Internet, Link**.
- The Application layer in TCP/IP soaks up OSI Layers 5, 6, and 7.
- The Internet layer maps to OSI Layer 3.
- The Link layer covers OSI Layers 1 and 2.
- This is what your operating system actually implements. OSI is the reference; TCP/IP is the shipped product.
