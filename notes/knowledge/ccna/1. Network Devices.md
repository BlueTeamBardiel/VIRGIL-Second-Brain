# Network Devices

## What it is

A network is a Discord server for machines — devices join, talk to each other, share files, voice-chat over VoIP, stream media, and request services. Every device that shows up and participates is called a **node**: routers, switches, firewalls, servers, your laptop, the smart fridge, all of it.

But just like a Discord server needs roles, channels, and moderators to not descend into chaos, a network needs specialized devices that each do one job well:

- **Router** — the bouncer between neighborhoods. Moves traffic between separate networks (your LAN ↔ the internet, or LAN A ↔ LAN B).
- **Switch** — the in-house traffic director. Moves Ethernet frames between devices *inside* the same LAN.
- **Firewall** — the security checkpoint. Inspects traffic and decides who gets through based on rules.
- **Server** — the host. Sits there waiting to answer requests (web pages, files, email).
- **Client** — the asker. Your phone hitting Spotify is a client requesting a service.

In Watch Dogs 2, when Marcus pivots through a corporate network, he's literally hopping node-to-node — every camera, laptop, and access point is a node with a role.

## Why it matters

Mixing these up is the networking equivalent of trying to use a kitchen knife as a screwdriver. A switch *cannot* route between networks — drop a switch where you needed a router and traffic between your 192.168.1.0/24 and 10.0.0.0/24 LANs goes nowhere. Skip the firewall and your network is a public lobby in Escape from Tarkov: anyone who finds the IP can take a shot at your services.

Knowing which device does what is also the foundation for every later topic — VLANs, ACLs, NAT, routing protocols. You can't reason about traffic flow if you don't know what each box on the diagram actually does.

## Key facts

### Router
- Directs packets between **distinct LANs** or between a LAN and the internet, using a **routing table** (the GPS of the network — every destination has a "best path" entry).
- Typically **4–8 ports**, point-to-point connections (each port talks to one other device, like a 1v1 in a fighting game).
- `no shutdown` — enables an interface. Cisco interfaces ship disabled; this is the "press start" command.
- `ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 [next-hop]` — sets the **default route**. Translation: "if I don't know where this packet goes, send it here." It's the fast-travel waypoint in Elden Ring when you're lost.

### Switch
- Connects devices **inside a single LAN**. Forwards **Ethernet frames** based on **MAC addresses** — it learns who lives on which port, like a DM remembering which player is in which voice channel.
- **Cannot route between networks.** No routing table. If a frame's destination is on a different LAN, the switch shrugs and the router has to step in.
- Each port is its own **collision domain** — meaning two devices on different ports can't step on each other's transmissions. Old hubs were one giant shared mic; switches give every player their own headset.
- Typically **24–48 ports**, multi-port connections.

### Firewall
- Inspects and filters traffic against **predefined security rules** — the bag-check before you board.
- Can sit **outside** the network (in front of the router, facing the internet) or **inside** the LAN (segmenting trusted zones from less-trusted ones, like Among Us where Electrical is locked off from Cafeteria).
- Typically **2–4 ports**, point-to-point or full-mesh.
- **Access lists (ACLs)** — rules that permit or deny traffic by protocol and port number. Example: permit TCP 443 to the web server, deny everything else. It's the loadout restriction in a custom Call of Duty lobby.

### Server vs Client
- **Server** — hosts a service (web, file, email, game server) and waits for requests. Think the Minecraft server everyone connects to.
- **Client** — initiates the request. Your Minecraft launcher is the client. The relationship is asymmetric: clients ask, servers answer.
- A single machine can play both roles depending on the conversation — your laptop is a client to Netflix but a server when you share files over LAN.

### Quick comparison

| Device | Operates on | Ports | Routes between LANs? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Router | IP addresses | 4–8 | Yes |
| Switch | MAC addresses | 24–48 | No |
| Firewall | Rules (IP/port/protocol) | 2–4 | Filters, doesn't primarily route |
